Our Story

It was never
really about paint.

The story of two people building a life — who found out the company was just one of the things they were building.

This isn't really the story of a painting company. It's the story of two people building a life — who found out somewhere along the way that the company was just one of the things they were building. A consequence of the story, not the story itself. And if I've learned one thing, it's that nothing here is arbitrary. Everything means something.

Where it starts

Four colors that weren't mine.

I grew up in a tense house — the kind where a kid learns early to find the one small piece of the world he can actually control. I found some of it in paint. When I was eight, I asked to paint my own room and got handed four colors I never chose. A couple of years later I spray-painted my bike in secret, just to have one thing that looked like me instead of like everyone else's expectations. I got punished for it.

What I didn't understand yet was that I'd already found the thing that would run through the rest of my life: when I changed the world around me, it changed how I felt inside it. That's where all of it begins — the eye for beauty, for order, for putting something right — long before there was ever a business to point it at.

The thread under everything

The fundamentals nobody claps for.

For a long time I was sure music was what I was actually for, and that painting houses was just what paid the bills until I got there. I fell in love with everything underneath a song — the symbolism, the pacing, the way a lyric could become a place you could stand inside. I started thinking cinematically: whole scenes running while I drove, songs turning into rooms, rooms turning into stories.

A guitar teacher named Pepper Brown taught me the thing that outlasted the music itself. Greatness isn't the flashy solo, he said. It's practicing the fundamentals ten thousand times when nobody is watching. I didn't know it then, but that one idea would become how I think about everything — how I'd build systems, run a business, notice what makes something beautiful and then build toward it on purpose.

The dream didn't die. The medium changed. The impulse under it never did.

Ale

Nine months.

Ale grew up a world away from me — born in Bolivia, raised in Italy from the time she was eight, in the United States by twenty-four, working as an au pair. That's when we met. Neither of us needed much convincing; we'd both grown up watching a parent start with nothing and refuse to quit until it became something real. So we already knew what building a life would cost.

Nine months after we met, we were married. We didn't have a plan. We had each other, and a conviction that whatever we made, we'd make it for real.

The beginning

The only way I could see.

The business wasn't really a plan. Ale had just moved in, and I'd just lost my job. Painting together was the only way I could see to make any of it work — to keep providing, and to keep her. It didn't start as ambition. It started as necessity: two people who needed each other to show up for a life we'd only just started building.

It didn't start as a company. It started as a way to keep her.

We shared a small room with roommates in Chester, Pennsylvania, and got to jobs in an $800 1998 Ford Econoline. Our first year finished about a thousand dollars in the hole — more went out than came in — kept alive by a small loan from Ale's father that we paid back inside the year. The future was completely uncertain. So were the first real lessons.

The $800 Econoline years.

What the work taught us

One bathroom.

One of the first lessons came from a woman on a fixed income who wanted a single bedroom painted, the rest "someday." Driving home, I caught myself wondering why she'd spend money on paint at all. The answer changed how I see the work: people aren't buying paint. They're trying to make the world around them match who they are inside. Leaving a lazy line isn't a small shortcut — it's telling someone they weren't worth the extra ten minutes.

Another customer hired us for one bathroom. What we didn't know was that it was a test — she never handed a big job to a company she hadn't tried first. We treated that bathroom like it was the whole house, so she gave us the whole house. Then her cabinets. Years later it had turned into an eight-year relationship and a real friendship. One bathroom, done like it mattered, opened a door we never could have seen coming.

Trust compounds. So does character.

Apart, then home

A year and a half.

Then Ale went to visit her family and couldn't get back — a visa issue turned a trip into a year and a half apart. The uncertainty never moved us. She came home on a Thursday. We moved into a little apartment in Chester on Friday. By the next week we were back to work, together. The story just kept going.

2020

Everything, the same week.

In the spring of 2020 the world shut its doors — and the same week, Ale told me she was pregnant. A quirk of timing had just left us out of the relief a lot of our competitors got; some of them bought new trucks with it while we kept working exactly as hard as before, on our own.

Music slipped quietly into the background. Responsibility took its place. For the first time, I understood I wasn't building work anymore. I was building a future.

Ezra

Help.

When our son was born, we named him Ezra on purpose — it means help — because that's exactly what it felt like we'd been given. The company stopped being an experiment the day he arrived. It became a responsibility with a face.

Ezra. It means "help."
Humility

The phone call that humbled me.

Craft and effort had carried us that far, and I could feel it wasn't going to be enough. I'd never managed anyone in my life. So I went looking for people who actually knew how to run a company — and got humbled fast by a phone call, an honest audit of my own business that showed me exactly how far from professional we really were.

I flew the family to a conference in Florida, came home ashamed of where we stood and lit up by what was possible, and spent the next few years learning — slowly, badly at first — how to build an actual organization instead of just good paint jobs.

Failure

A house full of dust.

Growth surfaces problems you never had before. On one job, while I was away, a piece of equipment failed mid-task. Instead of stopping to replace it, the crew improvised — and filled a family's house with dust, bad enough that they couldn't stay there with their infant. We paid for the hotel, the cleaning, and refunded the job well past our own costs. We lost money, and that family will probably never call us again.

But the rule that came out of it is absolute now: if equipment fails, you replace it immediately, whatever it costs, no exceptions. Protecting the profit on a job at the expense of someone's experience isn't smart business. It's the opposite of it.

Standards

Falta gente.

We learned the hardest lesson slowly: you can teach skill, but you can't teach character. A wave of new hires didn't work out, and I found myself nearly back to running everything alone. Around then Ale's father, Teofilo, was visiting from Bolivia and worked beside me on a huge job, telling me over and over in Spanish — falta gente. You need people. You need good people.

On his last day, a man named Virgilio came in to try out. He never left. To this day he's the only painter I'd put on my own level — not for raw skill, but because he understands, really understands, what we're trying to do here. He's been the standard for every hire since.

Skill can be taught. Character can't.

The part I don't usually say

The weight.

There were real moments — even after we had a good team, even after it looked like it was working — when I was scared enough to want to quit. That never fully went away. Early on, quitting would only have cost my own family. Later it would have cost the people who worked for me and their families too, and that changed everything about what I was willing to endure.

The other half was a stubborn conviction I couldn't put down. I could feel the ingredients were already there — passion, skill, care. What was missing wasn't talent. It was intentionality. Every time the urge to walk away showed up, the answer was never that it disappeared. It was that I had to find more courage than I felt like having, because it wasn't only my risk to carry anymore.

It had started as a way to keep one person in my life. It never stopped being that — the circle around it just kept getting bigger.

What it all came down to

Ordinary things, done extraordinarily well.

And there it was again — the thing Pepper Brown told me about a guitar, twenty years before I understood where it was going. Extraordinary isn't a grand gesture. It's ordinary things — covering the floor, communicating clearly, showing up the same way every single time — done consistently, deliberately, and exceptionally well.

The more I focused on the customer's actual experience instead of just the work, the easier everything else got — because I finally understood that everything else rose and fell on that one thing.

Alba

Dawn.

In 2023 we had a daughter. We named her Alba — dawn, sunrise — because that's what it felt like. One more reminder that nothing in our life had ever really been arbitrary.

What comes next

Why Quack?

Eventually the whole philosophy earned a name of its own. The duck was on the truck before it was ever a plan — the best for your nest — a small joke that turned out to say exactly what we mean. This is about your home, your people, the place you come back to.

Quack isn't us becoming something else. It's us finally naming what we've been the whole time. Same two people. Same standard.

The mission was never really to paint houses. It's to honor the trust a person places in a stranger who walks into their home — and leave things better than we found them. The same instinct an eight-year-old had with a can of spray paint, grown all the way up.
